# Pre-requisite (optional) — Fourier from scratch

*Everything about waves, frequencies and transforms that the rest of this
site assumes, and not one thing more. No calculus, no signal-processing
course, no prior exposure. If you already picture a spectrum comfortably,
skip this page.*

!!! tip "Who this is for, and how long it takes"

    Three kinds of reader:

    - **never studied it.** Start at §1 and read straight through. You need

## 1. A signal is just a list of numbers

Forget waves for a moment. A **signal** is a list of measurements taken in
order: the air pressure at your microphone every millisecond, the price of
something every day, the value of a function $f$ at each of $N$ inputs.
Write it as

$$
x[0],\ x[1],\ x[2],\ \dots,\ x[N-1] .
$$

That is all. When this site says "a function on $\mathbb{Z}_N$" it means
exactly this list, with the extra convention that after $x[N-1]$ it wraps
around to $x[0]$ again — the list lives on a circle rather than a line.

Two words you will meet constantly:

- **Time domain** — the list itself, in order. What you measured.
- **Frequency domain** — the same information rewritten as "how much of each
  repeating pattern is in there". What you are about to build.

Nothing is added or lost between the two. It is the same data in two
coordinate systems, like giving a location as "3 blocks east, 4 blocks
north" or as "5 blocks, at 53°". Some questions are trivial in one and
awkward in the other, which is the entire reason anyone bothers.

## 2. A frequency is a counted number of wiggles

The repeating patterns we will use are cosines and sines. The only thing you
need about them is the picture: a smooth up-and-down that repeats.

**Frequency** is how many complete up-and-downs fit in your window. If your
window is one second and the wave goes up and down 3 times, that is
frequency 3. Sampled at $N$ points, the wave of frequency $k$ is

$$
\cos\!\left(2\pi k \frac{x}{N}\right), \qquad x = 0, 1, \dots, N-1 .
$$

The $2\pi$ is a units nuisance — a full trip around a circle is $2\pi$
radians — and carries no idea. Ignore it and read the formula as **"$k$
complete cycles across the window"**.

Every signal you will meet on this site is a sum of such waves, each with an
**amplitude** (how big) and a **phase** (where it starts). Here is one built
from three:

  ![Three cosines and the signal they add up to, with its spectrum](fig-recipe.svg#only-light)
  ![Three cosines and the signal they add up to, with its spectrum](fig-recipe-dark.svg#only-dark)

  <code>scripts/make_primer_figures.py</code>.</figcaption>

!!! abstract "In one sentence"

    **A Fourier transform reads the recipe off the cake.** You are handed
    the sum; it tells you which waves went in and in what proportion.

Note the bar at frequency 0 in that figure. Frequency zero is the wave that
never wiggles — a flat line — so "how much of frequency 0" is just **the
average of the signal**. Remember that one; on the quantum pages it turns
into an entire algorithm.

## 3. The one measurement: multiply, then average

How do you find out whether a particular wave is hiding in a signal? You do
the only thing available:

!!! abstract "The recipe, in full"

    1. Take your signal.
    2. Multiply it, point by point, by the wave you are asking about (the
       **probe**).
    3. Average the result.

    That average is "how much of that wave is present". Repeat for every
    frequency and you have the whole spectrum.

Why does it work? If the signal contains the probe's wave, the two go up
together and down together, so their product is positive nearly everywhere
and the average is big. If the signal contains a *different* wave, the two
drift in and out of step, and the product spends exactly as much time
negative as positive — the average is zero. Not small. **Zero**, exactly, for
every pair of different whole-number frequencies.

Turn the dial yourself:

<div class="qq-anim" data-anim="tuner"></div>

<figure markdown="span">
  ![Matched and mismatched probes, and the swept spectrum](fig-correlation.svg#only-light)
  ![Matched and mismatched probes, and the swept spectrum](fig-correlation-dark.svg#only-dark)
  <figcaption>Blue area counts positive, amber counts negative. Matched
  probe: the blue wins and the average is 0.500. Mismatched: they balance to
  −0.000. Sweep every probe and the spectrum appears.</figcaption>
</figure>

This exact cancellation has a name — **orthogonality** — and it is not a
convenience. It is the entire engine, and on the quantum pages it is what
"interference" means. Every "the wrong answers cancel and the right one
survives" sentence you will ever read about a quantum algorithm is this
picture with different vocabulary.

!!! question "Stop and think"

    Why does the mismatched product cancel *exactly*, instead of just coming
    out small?

    ??? success "One answer"

        Because a whole number of cycles fits in the window. Multiply two
        cosines of different whole-number frequencies and trigonometry turns
        the product into a sum of two cosines, of frequencies $k+j$ and
        $k-j$ — both non-zero whole numbers, so each completes a whole
        number of cycles across the window, and a full cycle of a cosine
        averages to zero. Nothing is approximated anywhere.

        Keep this: the exactness comes from *whole numbers of cycles
        fitting*. In §7 we break that condition on purpose and watch the
        exactness die — which is precisely what happens to Shor's algorithm
        when a period does not divide the register size.

## 4. Why complex numbers show up (and why they are the easy way)

There is one wrinkle. A cosine and a sine of the same frequency are the same
wave shifted sideways, and a signal can contain either — or anything in
between. Probing with a cosine alone can therefore report "nothing here"
when the signal is full of that frequency, sitting a quarter-cycle out of
step.

The fix is to probe with both at once and keep the two answers as a single
number:

$$
e^{2\pi i k x / N} \;=\; \cos\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi k x}{N}\right)
\;+\; i \sin\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi k x}{N}\right) .
$$

That expression is not deep. **It is a point walking around a circle of
radius 1**, taking $N$ evenly spaced steps per lap, going round $k$ times.
The complex number is the bookkeeping device that lets one symbol carry both
"where on the circle" and "how far around".

The answer for each frequency is then one complex number, and its two parts
have plain meanings:

| the number's… | means |
|---|---|
| **magnitude** | how much of that frequency is present |
| **angle** | where the wave starts — its phase |

!!! abstract "The property that makes them worth the trouble"

    Delaying a wave multiplies it by a single number, $e^{2\pi i k a/N}$.
    In cosine-and-sine bookkeeping the same delay is a two-line identity
    that stirs sine into cosine. This "a shift becomes one multiplication"
    property is the whole reason complex exponentials became standard —
    and on the quantum pages, that single multiplying number is called a
    **character**, and it is also exactly what a **phase** on a qubit is.

    You do not need to be comfortable with complex arithmetic to read the
    rest of this site. You need to know that a complex number here is *a
    point on a circle*, and that multiplying by one *rotates*.

??? note "▸ deeper — the factor of two you will notice in the figures"

    A real cosine of amplitude $A$ shows up as **two** entries of magnitude
    $A/2$: one at frequency $+k$, one at $-k$ (equivalently $N-k$). It is
    the average of two counter-rotating circular waves, and each gets half.

    So "amplitude $A$" and "coefficient $A/2$" are both correct, depending
    on which you mean. The figures on this page plot amplitudes, because
    that is what "the recipe" should read like; the code returns
    coefficients. `test_a_real_cosine_splits_between_plus_and_minus_k` pins
    it so the two conventions can never silently disagree.

## 5. Putting it together: the discrete Fourier transform

That is the whole construction. Written out:

$$
X[k] \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{x=0}^{N-1}
\underbrace{\text{signal}[x]}_{\text{what you measured}}
\cdot
\underbrace{e^{-2\pi i k x/N}}_{\text{the probe wave}}
$$

Read it left to right in English: **for each frequency $k$, multiply the
signal by that probe wave and add up.** The $1/\sqrt{N}$ is a scaling
convention that makes the transform and its inverse symmetric; different
books put it in different places and nothing conceptual depends on it.

Three facts about it, all of which get used later and none of which need a
proof to be usable:

- **It is reversible.** Nothing is lost; you can rebuild the signal from its
  spectrum exactly (`test_round_trip`).
- **Energy is conserved** (Parseval): the total size of the signal equals
  the total size of the spectrum, $\sum_x |x[n]|^2 = \sum_k |X[k]|^2$
  (`test_parseval_energy_is_conserved`). On the quantum pages this is why
  measuring after a transform is legal — probabilities still add to one.
- **Shifting the signal never changes the magnitudes** — only the phases
  (`test_shifting_a_signal_only_moves_phases`). Move a sound earlier and it
  contains the same frequencies. This is the single most important fact on
  this page for what comes next, and the whole of deep dive 01 hangs off it.

!!! warning "The FFT is a matter of speed, not of meaning"

    Done directly, the transform costs $N$ multiplications for each of $N$
    frequencies: $N^2$ operations. The **Fast Fourier Transform** (1965, and
    Gauss in 1805) rearranges this into $N \log N$ by noticing that the even
    and odd samples share most of the work, and recursing.

    That is a *speed* trick and nothing else. Same answer, same meaning.
    When deep dive 01 says a quantum circuit performs "the same butterfly"
    with $\log N$ gates, this recursion is the butterfly it means — and the
    reason it is **not** a faster FFT is spelled out there, at length,
    because that misunderstanding has cost the field years.

## 6. A worked classic: the square wave

Worth doing once, because it makes "a shape is a sum of waves" concrete and
because its punchline reappears in the quantum material.

Take a signal that is $+1$ for half the window and $-1$ for the other half.
Feed it through §3 and the answer is: only **odd** frequencies are present,
with amplitude $4/\pi k$ — the even ones cancel exactly
(`test_square_wave_harmonics` measures this rather than quoting it).

Add them back one at a time:

<div class="qq-anim" data-anim="harmonics"></div>

<figure markdown="span">
  ![Partial sums of the square wave, and the Gibbs overshoot](fig-square.svg#only-light)
  ![Partial sums of the square wave, and the Gibbs overshoot](fig-square-dark.svg#only-dark)
  <figcaption>Left: 1, 3, 9 and 49 harmonics crawling toward a square. Right:
  the overshoot at the corner settles at 8.95% of the jump and stays there
  forever — the Wilbraham–Gibbs constant.</figcaption>
</figure>

Look at the corners. The partial sums overshoot the jump by about **9% of
its height**, and adding more terms does not shrink the overshoot — it only
makes it narrower. This is the **Gibbs phenomenon**, and the moral is worth
carrying:

!!! abstract "A finite recipe cannot make a sharp edge"

    Smooth ingredients can approximate a jump as closely as you like *in
    area*, but never *in height*. Sharpness in one domain costs spread in
    the other, always.

    That trade-off has many names — Gibbs ringing here, spectral leakage in
    §7, the uncertainty principle in physics, the Dirichlet kernel in deep
    dive 01 §8. They are one theorem seen from four directions.

## 7. Two consequences of having finitely many samples

Both of these come back on the quantum pages, so meet them here where they
are harmless.

### Aliasing: $N$ samples can only tell $N$ frequencies apart

Sample a wave of frequency 17 at 16 points and you get **literally the same
numbers** as a wave of frequency 1 (`test_aliased_frequencies_are_literally_the_same_samples`).
No algorithm can separate them, because there is nothing to separate — the
data is identical. Frequencies above $N/2$ fold back and masquerade as low
ones. This is why a fast wheel looks like it is turning backwards in a film,
and why a spectrum has a highest meaningful bin.

### Leakage: a tone that does not fit spreads out

§3's exact cancellation needed *whole numbers of cycles fitting the window*.
Take a tone at 8.5 cycles instead of 8, and nothing cancels perfectly
anywhere: its energy smears across every bin. At 8.0 cycles two bins hold
100% of the energy; at 8.5 cycles they hold 42%
(`test_a_fractional_frequency_smears`).

<figure markdown="span">
  ![Aliasing and spectral leakage](fig-sampling.svg#only-light)
  ![Aliasing and spectral leakage](fig-sampling-dark.svg#only-dark)
  <figcaption>Left: sixteen samples cannot tell 1 cycle from 17. Right, on a
  log scale: an exact frequency lives in one bin and nowhere else; a
  fractional one is present in every bin, forever.</figcaption>
</figure>

!!! tip "Where you will meet these again"

    **Leakage is the reason Shor's algorithm is hard to analyse.** The
    hidden period $r$ usually does not divide the register size $N$, so the
    spectrum leaks exactly as in that right-hand panel, and the algorithm
    has to work with a smeared peak instead of a sharp one. Deep dive 01 §8
    computes what that costs; the mechanism is the picture above and nothing
    more exotic.

## 8. The other identity worth knowing: convolution

One more, stated without proof because you only need to recognise it.

**Convolution** is the operation "slide one signal along another, multiply,
and add" — blurring, echoes, moving averages, and the probability
distribution of a sum of two random variables are all convolutions. It is
expensive and awkward in the time domain.

In the frequency domain it becomes **multiplication, bin by bin**. That is
it. The reason Fourier transforms are everywhere in engineering is largely
this one line: convolution is hard, multiplication is easy, and the
transform swaps one for the other (`test_convolution_theorem_over_z2n`,
over on deep dive 01's side).

Keep the general shape of it: **the transform turns the group's own
operation into ordinary multiplication.** In §9 the "group operation" stops
being addition and becomes XOR, and this sentence survives unchanged.

## 9. The bridge: what changes when the group is bit strings

Here is the hinge onto everything else on this site. Read this section
slowly; it is short.

Everything above used one structure: a set of positions you can **shift
around cyclically** — the integers $0, 1, \dots, N-1$ with wraparound, which
mathematicians call $\mathbb{Z}_N$. The waves were points stepping around a
circle.

Quantum registers are $n$ bits, and the natural "shift" on bit strings is
not "add one" — it is **XOR by a mask**: flip this set of bits. The set of
$n$-bit strings under XOR is a perfectly good group, written
$\mathbb{Z}_2^n$, and the whole construction above applies to it unchanged.
But one thing collapses:

!!! abstract "In a group where doing anything twice returns you to the start, a wave has only two values"

    A wave must send each group element to a point on the unit circle, and
    it must respect the group's operation. XOR-ing a mask with itself gives
    zero — every element is its own inverse — so every point the wave visits
    must satisfy $v^2 = 1$. On the unit circle only two numbers do that:
    $+1$ and $-1$.

    **So on bit strings, a "wave" is a pattern of plus and minus signs.**
    Not an approximation of one. That is the entire object.

<div class="qq-anim" data-anim="twovalues"></div>

Those sign patterns are written $(-1)^{x \cdot z}$, where $z$ is the "which
bits do I look at" mask that plays the role of frequency, and $x \cdot z$
counts how many of the selected bits are 1. Sweeping the probe over all $2^n$
masks is the same operation as §3, and the cancellation is just as exact:

<figure markdown="span">
  ![From cosines on Z_8 to sign patterns on Z_2^3](fig-bridge.svg#only-light)
  ![From cosines on Z_8 to sign patterns on Z_2^3](fig-bridge-dark.svg#only-dark)
  <figcaption>Left: on \(\mathbb{Z}_8\) a wave takes many values. Middle: on
  \(\mathbb{Z}_2^3\) the same waves have only ±1 available. Right: they are
  still exactly orthogonal — every mismatched pair cancels to zero.</figcaption>
</figure>

And now the payoff. Run §5's formula on the smallest interesting group,
$N = 2$ — one bit, two positions:

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}
$$

That matrix is the **Hadamard gate**, the first gate in nearly every quantum
circuit ever drawn. Nobody designed it to be a Fourier transform; it is the
2-point Fourier transform, and stacking $n$ of them side by side gives the
transform on $n$-bit strings (`test_two_point_dft_is_the_hadamard_gate`,
`test_walsh_patterns_are_the_z2n_transform`).

!!! abstract "What you now know"

    A layer of Hadamard gates — the thing every quantum algorithm starts and
    ends with — is doing precisely what §3 did: **multiplying by every probe
    wave and averaging, all at once**. Not by analogy. By identity.

    That is the sentence [deep dive 01](../01-fourier-thread/notes.md) is
    built on, and you are now equipped to read it.

## 10. Self-test

If you can answer these, the prerequisite has done its job. Answers are one
click away, but try first.

!!! question "1 · What is the Fourier coefficient at frequency zero?"

    ??? success "Answer"

        The average of the signal. The zero-frequency probe is a flat line,
        so multiplying by it changes nothing and you are left averaging the
        signal itself. **On the quantum side this single fact is the whole
        of the Deutsch–Jozsa algorithm** — it asks whether a function is
        constant or balanced, which is a question about its mean, which is
        the coefficient at frequency zero.

!!! question "2 · Why do mismatched frequencies cancel exactly, not approximately?"

    ??? success "Answer"

        Because both frequencies are whole numbers of cycles per window, so
        their product is again a whole number of cycles, and a full cycle
        averages to zero. Exactness comes from *fitting*. Break the fitting
        — a period that does not divide the window — and you get leakage
        (§7), which is exactly what makes Shor's analysis delicate.

!!! question "3 · A signal is delayed by three samples. What happens to its spectrum?"

    ??? success "Answer"

        The magnitudes are unchanged; only the phases rotate. The frequencies
        present in a sound do not depend on when it started. This is the
        property deep dive 01 elevates into its central claim: the Fourier
        basis is the basis in which *shifting* becomes multiplying by a
        single number, and that is why hidden periodicity is visible there
        and nowhere else.

!!! question "4 · Why does a wave on bit strings only take the values ±1?"

    ??? success "Answer"

        Because XOR-ing a mask with itself returns you to where you started,
        so every value $v$ the wave takes must satisfy $v^2 = 1$, and on the
        unit circle only $+1$ and $-1$ do. A "wave" on $\mathbb{Z}_2^n$ is
        therefore a pattern of signs — and the rows of the Hadamard layer
        are exactly those patterns.

!!! question "5 · An FFT computes a transform in N log N instead of N². Does a quantum computer therefore give you a faster FFT?"

    ??? success "Answer"

        No — and this is the most valuable thing on this page. A quantum
        circuit does perform the same butterfly with only $\log N$ gates,
        but you cannot load $N$ arbitrary numbers into the amplitudes for
        free, and you cannot read the $N$ output coefficients back out: one
        measurement returns *one* index, at random, with probability given
        by that coefficient squared.

        So the quantum version is not a faster FFT — it is a different
        instrument that answers a narrower question. Deep dive 01 §5 and §6
        make that precise, and it is the filter that explains why this
        mechanism has produced about four algorithms in forty years.
